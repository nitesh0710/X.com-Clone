If you run a trade business, you've probably been told two opposite things. One person says "nobody looks at websites anymore, everyone uses Google Maps." Another says "you're nobody without a website." Both are partly right, and the honest answer depends on how you get work today.

Here's how I think about it.

## What your Google listing does well

A Google Business Profile (the listing that shows up on Maps and in the box on the right of search results) is free, and it's powerful. For a lot of local searches, it's the first thing a customer sees.

It shows your name, phone number, hours, reviews and photos. Customers can tap to call or get directions without ever leaving Google. For many trades, a well-kept profile with plenty of good reviews brings in steady calls on its own.

So if you don't have a Google Business Profile, start there. It's the single most useful free thing you can set up. A website doesn't replace it.

## What a Google listing can't do

The trouble is that your profile looks almost exactly like everyone else's. Same layout, same buttons, same little boxes. When a customer is choosing between three electricians with similar ratings, the profile doesn't give them much to separate you.

Here's what a profile struggles with:

**Explaining what you actually do.** You can list services, but you can't explain them. A homeowner wondering whether you handle panel upgrades, EV chargers or just repairs often can't tell from a profile.

**Showing up for specific searches.** When someone searches "water heater replacement in [town]" or "EV charger installer near me," Google looks at websites as well as profiles. A site with a page about that exact job gives Google something to match. A profile alone often doesn't.

**Looking established.** Fair or not, a lot of customers check whether a business has a real website before they call, especially for bigger jobs. No website, or a broken one, makes some people wonder whether you're still in business.

**Taking a proper quote request.** Profiles have messaging, but it's easy to miss and hard to manage. A website form can ask the right questions (what's the job, where, how urgent) and send them straight to your phone and email.

**Belonging to you.** Google controls your profile. Listings get suspended by mistake, features get removed, and the layout changes whenever Google decides. Your website and your domain are yours.

## How customers actually use both

In practice, most people use both without thinking about it. They search, see a few profiles on the map, then tap through to the websites of the two or three that look promising. They look at photos, check that you cover their area and see whether you do the job they need. Then they call.

That means your profile gets you onto the shortlist, and your website helps you win the job. The two work best when they point at each other: the profile links to the website, and the website shows the same name, phone number and service area as the profile.

## When you can probably skip a website (for now)

I build websites, so take this with that in mind, but there are cases where a website won't change much:

- You're fully booked months ahead from referrals and repeat customers, and you don't want more work.
- You work almost entirely for one or two general contractors who never look you up.
- You're winding the business down.

If that's you, keep your Google profile tidy, ask happy customers for reviews and save your money.

## When a website is worth paying for

A simple website is usually worth it when:

- **You want more of a particular kind of job.** If you want more panel upgrades and fewer small repairs, a page about panel upgrades helps you show up for those searches.
- **You're competing with businesses that have good sites.** If the electricians around you all have clean websites and you don't, you're starting every comparison behind.
- **Your jobs are bigger.** The more a job costs, the more research a customer does before calling, and the more a website matters.
- **You get a lot of "are you still open?" questions.** That's a sign customers can't find enough about you.
- **You miss calls and lose the job.** A website with a quote form gives customers a way to reach you when you can't pick up.

## What "a website" should mean for a trade business

You don't need anything fancy. A good trade website does a small number of things well:

1. A phone number you can tap, on every screen.
2. A short quote form that sends requests to your phone and email.
3. A page for each main service, written in plain words.
4. A list of the towns you cover.
5. Real photos of your work, if you have them.
6. Fast loading on a phone, because that's where most customers are.
7. The same name, phone and address as your Google profile, so Google trusts both.

That's it. No blog you'll never update, no animations, no chat bot you didn't ask for.

## The honest bottom line

A Google listing gets you found. A website helps you get chosen, lets you show up for the specific jobs you want, and gives you something Google can't take away. For most trade businesses that want to grow, it's worth having both, as long as the website is simple, fast and built to get calls.
